From briefing to delivery in 6 months: the system that put the 2014 World Cup security forces on the same screen

Overview

I coordinated the design of the system for the Integrated Command and Control Centers (CICCs) of the 2014 FIFA World Cup, used by security forces in the 12 host cities to operate together in real time.

The system was built by Módulo Security GRC, a Brazilian governance, risk and compliance software company. From discovery to final testing, my team designed the platform used in the centers and the tablet interface for field agents. The 2 mobile apps went to another company, and I handed them the design direction and the foundation of Módulo's visual identity.

By the numbers

  • 12 host cities

    with the system in operation

  • 6 months

    from briefing to design delivery

  • 3 profiles

    of operators on a single platform

The problem

During the World Cup, Brazil's Military, Civil, Federal and Federal Highway Police, the fire department, municipal guards, the public health service (SUS) and other agencies would have to share the same command rooms. Until then, each was used to working with its own system, methods, protocols and vocabulary.

The new system had to handle the planned agenda, such as a stadium inspection or the arrival of a delegation. It also had to respond to the unexpected, like a crash on Avenida Presidente Vargas, in downtown Rio, that required dispatching an ambulance and firefighters on the spot. There, a usability flaw could delay a response.

The design challenge was to create a common workflow for institutions that had never operated together, simple enough to use under pressure and accepted by all of them.

Constraints

  • Time: 6 months from briefing to design delivery, within an 8-month project. The last 2 went to installation at the centers, testing and team training. The World Cup date wasn't moving.
  • Shifting scope: the project started without a fully approved scope, and new features arrived mid-work.
  • A team to build: when the contract was signed, design was me and two other people. Within a month and a half, I hired four more, a UI designer, a UX designer and two front-end developers, and still had to onboard and train them before the project started.
  • Integration: the system ran on top of Risk Manager, Módulo's existing software, and part of what we designed had to connect to it.

Users

  • Call takers
  • Dispatchers
  • Administrators

The three profiles shared the same room and used the same system, each responsible for one stage of the operation. There were other profiles, but these were the main ones.

They handled two kinds of demand. Incidents (anything unscheduled) came in constantly: someone feeling sick, an accident, a crime. Events were scheduled in advance, and each one broke down into activities. A stadium inspection, for example, required booking the bomb squad and confirming the venue was available.

An incident always started on the street. After the crash on Presidente Vargas, the agent alerted the CICC by phone, radio or chat.

The call taker received the alert and routed it to the dispatcher of the responsible force, depending on whether the incident called for an ambulance, a police car or even a helicopter.

That force's dispatcher saw the nearest vehicles on the map, checked which were available and which were already responding, and sent the chosen one with a single click. It was the most critical profile, with seconds to decide.

The administrator, general or per force, coordinated the room and followed the whole operation on the video wall. For events, the CICC followed the schedule, tracked activities and checked whether assigned vehicles were on their way.

Since each profile saw a different part of the operation, the same screen reconfigured itself per profile, showing each one only what their role required.

Role andresponsibilities

I was Módulo's design and front-end coordinator on the project. I led the work from discovery with the agencies through user testing, set the interface direction and was the bridge to the agencies, the project's steering committee and Módulo's leadership.

Around 2011, Módulo had moved from waterfall to Scrum and from discipline-based teams to cross-functional ones. There weren't enough UX, UI and front-end people for each of them. So I organized my team in a rotation across Scrum teams: everyone sat together in the middle of the floor, each tied to one team but available to be lent to another as sprints required.

The model met resistance from both sides. The IT director feared the rotation would look like a step back in the Scrum transition, but I explained the reasoning and he trusted the proposal. Then the teams complained. I showed them that with a fixed person, each team would have a UX, UI or front-end specialist, but would be uncovered in any sprint that needed a different discipline. With the rotation, no team would go unsupported. Once the resistance passed, the model worked for everyone.

Team: Livia Mansur and Rebeca Silva (UX), Eduardo Rocha and PC (UI), Adriano Caheté and Marcus Carnevale (front-end).

Process

With a World Cup deadline and an unstable scope, the team followed a mantra: 100% functional first, beautiful later. Without access to the CICC operators, we tested twice with Módulo's internal users who already worked in call taking and dispatch: first on wireframes of the critical areas, then on the final layouts. The tests surfaced usability and vocabulary issues while still in wireframe, when fixing them took hours. Aesthetics only came in once the flow was validated. Three decisions held the project together.

  1. 1. Create a workflow both agencies and committee would approve

    What I did
    within the deadline, I could only interview two forces: SUS and the Civil Police. With them, I mapped processes, protocols and vocabularies, and the differences showed up right away. SUS opened a case with a short title, just to dispatch the ambulance, and filled in the description later. The Civil Police put the what, where and who into the title itself, like "bar fight with stabbing on Rio Branco", and never filled in the description. From there, I designed a common operating workflow. When the event-scheduling flow stalled in approval, I flew to Brasília to present it in person to the project's steering committee.
    Why
    there was no single user. Adopting one force's process as the standard was never on the table: the system would be the meeting point of different institutions, and a workflow imposed without listening would be rejected in operation. Scheduling had already been presented twice, focused on scope rather than design, and came back both times. The committee's objections needed design answers, face to face.
    Result
    the agencies began operating in the same workflow. In Brasília, the committee accepted the proposals I presented at the meeting, and the final scheduling screen, designed back at the office, was approved.

  2. 2. Design for a scope that kept changing

    What I did
    I made the map a permanent background and put each feature in an independent floating widget, shown or hidden according to the profile. The reference came from Google Maps, which already used a side panel over the map.
    Why
    I considered fixed screens per profile but dropped the idea before drawing it: every new requirement would mean redoing all of them. With widgets, a change became a permissions setting. If a dispatcher was now allowed to send text messages, we just enabled the SMS widget for them, with no interface redesign.
    Result
    the system absorbed scope changes without structural rework.

  1. 3. Make the map readable without a click

    What I did
    I created the pins' visual language, organized into 4 groups (events, units, points of interest and routes). Each pin showed the object type, like a fire truck or a police helicopter, and an "eyebrow" showed its status: en route, responding, available or out of service. Event pins pointed left and unit pins pointed right, so when an ambulance reached an incident, both appeared side by side instead of one covering the other. For the dispatcher, I designed the Dispatch Dashboard, which brought vehicle and incident status, radio, chat and address search into a single workstation.
    Why
    dispatchers decided under pressure, and every screen switch delayed the response to an incident. The pins met resistance: in 2013, map apps weren't yet part of everyday life, and many people found the design odd and asked for changes. I kept the proposal because before looking good, a pin had to be readable from a distance.
    Result
    after several adjustments, dispatchers could read the operation's status at a glance, even with overlapping pins, and coordinate resources from a single panel.

Learnings

  • In systems with multiple institutions, design brokers an agreement before drawing screens.
  • When scope is uncertain, the answer is modular architecture: each new requirement fits without redoing the whole.
  • In crisis interfaces, iconography is an information system.

I also learned that method isn't a luxury of loose deadlines. Both test rounds fit in 6 months because the modular architecture took rework out of the way.

What I'd do differently: it was my first government project, and I waited too long for answers that never came. Many important decisions stalled because no one felt authorized to take the risk of deciding. Today I'd make those calls earlier and move on, because that time was missed on the deadline. I'd also explain the rotation to the teams more patiently from the start. I did talk to them, but the resistance that came later shows the conversation needed to be bigger.

During the games, every vehicle on the map was a team on the street.
